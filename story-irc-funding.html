Stories from the work

When the rules changed mid-year, I went to our funders first

At the International Rescue Committee in San José, California, I was responsible for delivering a City of San José workforce and housing contract for refugee, asylee and unhoused families. Families came to us after displacement from Afghanistan, Ukraine, Syria, El Salvador and beyond, often with skills and credentials that didn't yet count in a new country.

The work covered the full path to stability: job readiness, placement with employers, housing, and help navigating benefits and public systems. I formed partnerships with employers who hired our clients, including DoorDash, Uber, Amazon, Walmart and Bloom Energy. Over two years, the program supported more than 1,200 households.

When referrals surged during the Afghan evacuation, I designed a triage process that sorted every referral into one of three tracks: urgent housing, ready to work, or benefits first. I ran a weekly stand-up to keep the team moving together. We enrolled more than 100 clients a week, and our quarterly reporting to the city stayed on track.

I also ran a donated-software program. I decided which participants received the software, led workshops on using it in daily life and at work, and tracked and reported how it was used.

Then, midway through the year, a state policy change tightened eligibility. Many families we had planned to serve no longer qualified, and our contract targets were suddenly out of reach.

I didn't wait for the quarter to close and report a shortfall. I redirected outreach toward households that still qualified, and I went to our funders early with three things: what had changed, what our first year had already achieved, and revised targets we could stand behind.

They didn't just stay with us. Program funding doubled, from roughly $250,000 to $500,000.

What it taught me
  • Tell partners early. Funders back grantees who bring a plan before the numbers slip, not a shortfall after.
  • Evidence is leverage. A clear record of outcomes can turn a setback into a case for greater investment.
  • Rigid targets need room to adapt. When policy shifts mid-year, the strongest funder–grantee partnerships reset targets together instead of penalizing the shortfall.
