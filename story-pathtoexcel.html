Building ed-tech that 35,000 children actually used

Most education technology doesn't fail because the software is weak. It fails because it was never built for the people in the room: a teacher with thirty children and forty minutes, and a six-year-old who would rather be playing.

At PathToExcel, I was the product manager for a game-based learning platform for kindergarten through third grade, leading both product and curriculum. I came to the role as a former teacher and a trained computer science engineer, so I understood both the user and the build.

Three product decisions followed from that.

Design for the real workflow. We built around how teachers actually run a school day, not how software assumes they do.

Make engagement the engine. Young children learn through play, so we turned more than 100 hours of standards-aligned lessons into games they chose to come back to.

Serve every stakeholder. Students, teachers and parents each needed something different from the product, and we designed for all three.

But a product only matters if it's adopted, so I treated adoption as part of the product. I onboarded schools district by district, including Evergreen School District in San José, trained teachers until they used the platform with confidence, and built partnerships with school districts and corporate partners. Behind the scenes, I kept product and content teams in the US and India shipping as one.

The result: more than 35,000 young learners across five school districts, and 25% growth in enrollment.

What it taught me
  • Start in the classroom, not the code. The deepest product insight comes from people who have lived the user's day.
  • Engagement is the outcome engine. When users want to come back, learning follows.
  • Adoption is the product. Distribution, training and trust decide whether great technology ever reaches the people it was built for.
